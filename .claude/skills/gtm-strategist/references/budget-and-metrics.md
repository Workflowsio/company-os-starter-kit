# Budget and metrics

Read at step 5, and whenever somebody asks whether a number is good. Every threshold here is a starting point. Replace it with your own once you have enough volume to be honest, and write the date next to the number when you do.

---

## The 70/20/10 split

- **70 percent** on the primary motion, meaning the one that works or the one your deal size and stage say is most likely to.
- **20 percent** on the secondary motion, the one you are building toward.
- **10 percent** on experiments: new channels, new tools, new approaches.

Review the split quarterly. When something in the 10 percent starts producing, promote it to the 20 and move something down. The split fails when nobody ever demotes anything, at which point you have four primary motions and no budget.

## Budget by stage

| Stage | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| **Pre-revenue** | Roughly 70 percent tools, being a CRM, a sequencer and basic enrichment. 20 percent on a premium social seat and content. 10 percent testing one paid channel. Nothing on headcount, because the founder does the selling and that is the point of this stage. |
| **Early traction** | Roughly 50 percent tools, adding signal detection and social automation. 30 percent on the first go-to-market hire, part-time or contract. 20 percent content and experiments. |
| **Scaling** | Roughly 40 percent headcount, one or two dedicated people. 30 percent tools, the full stack. 20 percent paid channels. 10 percent events or partnerships. |
| **Established** | Roughly 50 percent headcount, a full team. 25 percent tools. 15 percent paid acquisition. 10 percent events, sponsorships and partnerships. |

### Tool spend guardrails

- **Pre-revenue:** keep total tool spend small enough that cancelling everything is a one-day decision. A free CRM, a free enrichment tier and one paid sequencer is a viable starting stack.
- **Early:** if tool spend is rising faster than pipeline, you are over-tooled. Audit before adding.
- **Scaling and beyond:** budget per go-to-market employee per month rather than per tool, and audit quarterly for overlap. Two tools doing enrichment is the most common overlap and the easiest to miss, because both invoices are small.

The test for any purchase: which manual process does this replace, and what is the return. If that has no answer, it is not a tool decision, it is a hope.

## Leading indicators, tracked weekly

These say whether the engine is healthy now. When they go red, pipeline dries up four to eight weeks later, which is why they are worth watching weekly rather than monthly.

| Metric | Good | Acceptable | Investigate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outbound volume | enough to learn from | steady | too low to produce a signal |
| Open rate | above 50% | 30 to 50% | below 30%, which is deliverability rather than copy |
| Reply rate | above 5% | 2 to 5% | below 2%, meaning copy, targeting or list quality |
| Positive reply rate | above 2% | 1 to 2% | below 1%, meaning the message does not resonate |
| Social connection rate | above 30% | 15 to 30% | below 15%, meaning the profile or the targeting |
| Social reply rate | above 15% | 8 to 15% | below 8%, meaning message quality or timing |
| Meetings booked per week | on target | trending up | flat or declining three weeks running |
| Meeting show rate | above 80% | 60 to 80% | below 60%, meaning qualification or the confirmation process |
| Bounce rate | below 2% | 2 to 5% | above 5%, meaning stop sending and clean the list |

## Lagging indicators, tracked monthly or quarterly

These say whether the motion produces revenue. Leading indicators can look excellent while these deteriorate, which is what a qualification problem looks like from the outside.

| Metric | Good | Acceptable | Investigate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer acquisition cost | scales with deal size, see below | stable | rising two quarters running |
| Lifetime value to acquisition cost | above 3:1 | 2:1 to 3:1 | below 2:1, meaning the unit economics are broken |
| Payback period | under 12 months | 12 to 18 months | over 18 months, which is a cash flow risk |
| Pipeline velocity | increasing quarter on quarter | stable | declining, see the formula below |
| Win rate from opportunity | above 25% | 15 to 25% | below 15%, meaning qualification or sales process |
| Sales cycle length | stable or shortening | stable | lengthening, meaning complexity or competitive pressure |
| Net revenue retention | above 110% | 100 to 110% | below 100%, meaning churn is beating expansion |

### Acquisition cost by deal size

The ratio matters more than the number. A rough working rule: acquisition cost around a fifth of first-year contract value at the low end, and up to roughly a third at the high end where cycles are long and lifetime value is large. Anything above a third needs the lifetime value to justify it explicitly, in writing, rather than by assumption.

### Pipeline velocity

```
Pipeline velocity = (qualified opportunities x average deal size x win rate) / average cycle in days
```

Worked: 40 opportunities x 25,000 x 20 percent win rate / 60 days = 3,333 per day of expected revenue.

Track it monthly. When it declines, the formula tells you which of the four variables moved, which is more useful than the headline. A velocity drop caused by a longer cycle is a different problem from one caused by a lower win rate, and the two have opposite fixes.
