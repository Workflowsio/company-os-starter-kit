# Hooks and CTAs

Read while writing the first line and the last line. The hook decides whether the post is read at all, and the CTA decides whether anything happens afterwards.

---

## The twelve hook formulas

**1. Bold claim plus number**
- Template: "[specific number] + [specific result] + [timeframe]."
- Example: "We booked 320 meetings in October."
- Best for case studies and results posts, where the number does the credibility work.

**2. Contrarian statement**
- Template: "[common belief] is [wrong / broken / overrated]."
- Example: "Your CRM is lying to you."
- Best for hot takes. It has to be defensible rather than merely provocative.

**3. Story intro, starting in the middle**
- Template: "I [past tense verb] [specific detail] [time marker]."
- Example: "I applied for 300 jobs. Not a single reply."
- Best for origin stories and pivots. Drop the reader into the action rather than the setup.

**4. News hook**
- Template: "[company or platform] just [action]. Here is [what it means]."
- Example: "They just shipped an agent platform. Here is what changes for GTM teams."
- Best for reactive content, and only inside 24 to 48 hours.

**5. System reveal**
- Template: "I am giving away [specific asset]."
- Example: "I am giving away our entire outbound system. For free."
- Best for giveaways. The more specific the asset, the better it performs.

**6. Stat plus implication**
- Template: "[number]. [number]. [number]. [what it adds up to]."
- Example: "11,800 emails sent. 10.2 percent reply rate. 178 opportunities."
- Best for campaign breakdowns. Three or more numbers build momentum on their own.

**7. Before and after**
- Template: "[bad starting state]. [another bad detail]. That was [time period] ago."
- Example: "No pipeline. No team. That was seven months ago."
- Best for milestones. The contrast does all the work.

**8. Direct question**
- Template: "How many [things] are actually [doing what they claim]?"
- Example: "How many tools in your stack are actually contributing to pipeline?"
- Best for thought leadership. The question has to be uncomfortable, because a comfortable one gets scrolled past.

**9. Admission of failure**
- Template: "This [thing] [failed]. Here is [what changed]."
- Example: "This campaign flopped. 2,000 emails, 3 replies. Here is what went wrong."
- Best for lessons learned, and only when the failure is specific and the lesson is real.

**10. Pattern interrupt**
- Template: "Stop [thing everyone does]." or a two or three word statement.
- Example: "Stop building funnels."
- Best for hot takes. Shorter is better, and it has to be followed by a real argument.

**11. Curiosity gap**
- Template: "The [superlative] [thing] I ever [did] was [surprising detail]."
- Example: "The highest-converting email I ever sent was 19 words."
- Best for frameworks and case studies. The pull comes from the gap between expectation and detail.

**12. Social proof stack**
- Template: "[number] [units]. [number] [units]. [one result]."
- Example: "200 companies. 12 months. One playbook."
- Best for credibility posts and framework intros, compressing proof into a small space.

---

## Hook rules

- The first line stands alone. It has to stop somebody mid-scroll without the rest of the post.
- No emojis in a hook, ever.
- No question that can be answered with a no and a scroll. "Do you want to improve your marketing?" is a scroll.
- Specific beats vague. 320 meetings beats hundreds of meetings, and 19 words beats a short email.
- The hook is a promise, and the post has to deliver it. If the hook says here is the system, there is a system in the post.
- Write three hooks before picking one, and show the two you discarded. The author overrules you more often than you would expect, and they are usually right about their own voice.

## The five CTA types

Match the CTA to the goal. One CTA per post.

**1. Comment trigger.** Goal: reach and lead capture.
- Pattern: "Comment [KEYWORD] and I will send [specific thing]."
- Use for giveaways, frameworks and playbooks. Pair it with something that actually delivers the asset, because an undelivered promise costs more than the post gained.
- It works because it is one word to type and it creates visible engagement.

**2. Save prompt.** Goal: bookmarks and long-term visibility.
- Pattern: "Save this for [specific future situation]."
- Use for tool reviews, checklists and reference posts.

**3. Polarizing question.** Goal: comments and debate.
- Pattern: a specific question that forces a side.
- Example: "Is cold email dead, or are most people just bad at it?"
- Use for hot takes and news reactions. The question needs two clear sides, because "what do you think about marketing" generates nothing.

**4. Shared experience.** Goal: community and relatability.
- Pattern: "Anyone else [been through this]?"
- Example: "Anyone else had a campaign that looked perfect on paper and completely bombed?"
- Use for story, failure and milestone posts.

**5. Follow prompt.** Goal: follower growth.
- Pattern: "Follow for [specific content category]."
- Use only when the post itself proves the expertise. An empty follow prompt is ignored and slightly costly.

## CTA anti-patterns

| Pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Agree? Thoughts?" | Nothing about it makes anybody want to respond |
| "Like and share if you found this helpful" | Begging, and it signals low confidence in the content |
| "Link in comments" with no link | A broken promise trains the audience to distrust the next one |
| "Drop a comment below" | Gives the reader no topic and no reason |
| A question too broad to answer | "What do you think about B2B?" gets nothing. Ask about one specific thing |
| Two or three CTAs at once | Comment and follow and share means none of them |
