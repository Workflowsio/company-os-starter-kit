# SPARK, subject lines and the seven archetypes

Read before writing any first draft. SPARK is the structural backbone of one email. The archetype decides what that email is about.

---

## SPARK

Five elements, each with one job. If an element is not doing its job, cut it rather than padding it.

### S - Subject line

Pattern interrupt. Earn the open without misleading.

Three to five words, always lowercase, referencing their world rather than yours. No brackets, no exclamation marks, no emojis, and no fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" prefixes, which readers see through and which cost you the trust you were about to ask for.

- Good: `{{company}} + [topic]`, `quick question on [their process]`, `idea for {{company}}`
- Bad: `Exciting Opportunity for {{company}}!` screams marketing. `Introduction - [Your Company]` is about you. `Quick question` is so overused it carries no information.

### P - Personalized opening

Prove you did homework, and create the feeling that this person understands my world.

The opening has to fail the "could I send this to a thousand people" test. If it would survive unchanged, it is not personalization.

Good:
- "Saw {{company}} just opened a second office in Austin. That kind of expansion usually means [relevant pain] shows up about a quarter later."
- "Noticed you are hiring three SDRs right now. When teams scale that fast, [problem] tends to appear around month two."
- "A VP Sales at another [industry] company told me last week that [specific pain]. Curious whether you are seeing the same."

Bad:
- "I came across {{company}} and was impressed by your growth." Says nothing specific.
- "As a fellow [industry] professional..." Nobody talks like this.
- "I hope this email finds you well." Instant delete.
- "I noticed your company is in the [industry] space." You read their profile, and so what.

### A - Agitate the problem

Name the specific pain in their language. They should think "that is exactly my situation", not feel attacked.

Good:
- "Most teams at your stage are running outbound by hand, with reps spending two or three hours a day on prospecting instead of selling. Pipeline looks thin even when everyone is working hard."
- "The usual answer is to hire more reps, and at fully loaded cost that math stops working somewhere around rep four."

Bad:
- "Many companies struggle with sales efficiency." Vague enough to be about anything.
- "You must be frustrated with your current process." Presumptuous, and you do not know how they feel.
- "In today's competitive landscape..." Filler.

Rules: use the buyer's vocabulary rather than yours, so if they say pipeline you do not say revenue generation. Agitate the cost of inaction rather than the problem, because "what is this costing you a quarter" lands harder than "this is a problem". Keep it to two or three sentences, because you are naming the pain rather than lecturing about it.

### R - Relevant value

One concrete outcome, with a number. Not features, not a product tour.

Good:
- "We helped [similar company] add 2.1M in pipeline in 90 days without hiring a rep."
- "Three [industry] companies moved over this quarter, and the average result was 47 percent more qualified meetings from the same list size."

Bad:
- "Our platform helps you automate your outbound." A feature, not an outcome.
- "We provide end-to-end sales solutions." Meaningless.
- "Companies like yours see great results." No proof, no number, no specificity.

Rules: one proof point per email, because the reader needs one compelling reason to reply rather than a menu. Specific numbers always, so 47 percent beats nearly half and 847K beats nearly a million. Name a real company where you have permission, because a named peer beats "a company like yours".

### K - Kick-off CTA

A low-friction question that earns a reply. Not a calendar link, and not a meeting request. It should cost the reader under ten seconds.

Good:
- "Are you seeing the same thing?" Binary and easy.
- "Would it be worth sharing how [similar company] solved this?" Offers value rather than a pitch.
- "Is this even on the radar for this quarter?" Gives an easy out, which raises replies rather than lowering them.
- "Would it be a terrible idea to send the playbook over?" Invites a safe no.

Bad:
- "Let's hop on a quick 15-minute call." Too much commitment for a first email.
- "Are you free Thursday at 2pm?" Presumptuous and high friction.
- "Would you be open to a brief demo?" You have not earned a demo yet.
- "Let me know your thoughts." Not a question, and no clear action.

CTA by step:

| Step | CTA type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Confirm the problem exists | "Seeing this too?" |
| 2 | Offer a specific deliverable | "Want me to send the comparison?" |
| 3 | Micro-commitment or new angle | "Would a two-minute walkthrough be useful?" |
| 4 | Breakup with numbered options | "1. Bad timing 2. Wrong person 3. Not interested" |

Total length 60 to 120 words, 150 absolute maximum. If the whole email does not fit on a phone screen without scrolling, it is too long.

## Subject line formulas

Write ten before picking one. The first idea is rarely the best one.

| Formula | Example | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| `[deliverable] for {{company}}` | `growth playbook for acme` | You have something specific to offer |
| `{{company}} + [topic]` | `acme + outbound` | General opener, works for most campaigns |
| `quick [niche] question` | `quick hiring question` | Your opener is a genuine question |
| `[trigger event]` | `series b congrats` | Signal-based campaigns |
| `idea for {{company}}` | `idea for acme` | Creative Ideas archetype |
| `[peer company] mentioned you` | `[peer] mentioned you` | Referral or name-drop campaigns, only when true |
| `[metric] in [timeframe]` | `47% more pipeline in 90 days` | Strong proof point campaigns |
| `not another [category] pitch` | `not another crm pitch` | Self-aware pattern interrupt |
| `have you given up on [topic]?` | `have you given up on outbound?` | Invites a safe no |
| `[their problem] solved?` | `rep ramp time solved?` | Problem-aware audiences |

Always lowercase, three to five words, no special characters, and never your own company name. The subject is about them. Test subject lines in pairs and never more than two at a time.

## The seven archetypes

One primary archetype per campaign, chosen by the tree in the skill body.

| # | Name | Best for | Structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Observation** | A visible signal: hiring, funding, a leadership change | Signal you noticed, what it implies, what peers did, "seeing the same?" |
| 2 | **The Problem-Solution** | A well-understood pain with hard metrics | Name the problem, quantify the cost, how you solve it, specific proof, gap-hinting CTA |
| 3 | **The Referral Ceiling** | Founder-led companies growing by word of mouth | Acknowledge the growth, name the limitation, contrast with a predictable alternative, offer the playbook |
| 4 | **The Creative Ideas** | Prospects you can generate specific ideas for | "Spent time looking at {{company}}", two or three tailored ideas, "think any of these could work?" |
| 5 | **The Benchmark** | Data-driven buyers, usually VP and director level | Industry data point, how they likely compare, what the top quartile does, offer to run the comparison |
| 6 | **The Case Study** | You have a perfect-match customer story | One-line result, what their situation was before, what changed, "sound familiar?" |
| 7 | **The Whole Offer** | No signal available and the value prop stands alone | One-sentence credibility, specific result with proof, relevance bridge, "open to hearing more?" |

**Creative Ideas outperforms the rest when the ideas are genuinely specific**, and it is the one archetype most teams skip, because it needs real research per account. That is the whole reason it still works.

**Whole Offer is the named fallback rather than a first choice.** When you use it, say so in the handover, because a campaign running on the fallback is telling you the signal layer is missing rather than that the copy is weak.
