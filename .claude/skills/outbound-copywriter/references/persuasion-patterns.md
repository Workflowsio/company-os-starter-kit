# Power patterns and the calibrated-question layer

Read while drafting, once the archetype is chosen. SPARK gives the email its skeleton. These give it a reason to be read twice. Layer at least two into any email, and never more than three, because a message carrying five techniques reads like a technique.

---

## The seven power patterns

### 1. Question opener beats statement opener

Do not state a fact, ask a question that opens a gap.

- Weak: "Looks like you carry a solid catalog."
- Strong: "Are your current suppliers covering 100 percent of your inventory needs?"
- Why: a question forces the reader to self-assess before deciding whether to reply. A fact is easy to dismiss, and an open loop is not.

### 2. Peer story beats generic discovery

Open with what a similar company told you rather than how you found them.

- Weak: "I came across {{company}} while researching companies in [industry]."
- Strong: "Just spoke to another [industry] VP in [region]. They wanted to scale outbound and told me rep ramp time was eating the unit economics."
- Why: a peer story carries credibility, states the pain in the buyer's own language rather than your marketing language, and raises the question of what their peers are doing that they are not.

### 3. Reason-why framing

Do not pitch. Explain why somebody else came to you.

- Weak: "We have a new program for companies like yours."
- Strong: "The reason they came to us is that most agencies charge per seat. We charge per meeting booked."
- Why: it flips the frame from selling to being bought, and third-party validation gets processed differently from a direct claim.

### 4. Specific numbers beat ranges

32 percent sounds measured, and 30 to 40 percent sounds like a guess.

- Weak: "The average gap has been 30 to 40 percent."
- Strong: "The average gap has been 32 percent."
- More: "847K" not "nearly a million". "14 days" not "about two weeks". "23 companies" not "dozens".
- Why: a precise number signals that somebody ran the analysis. A range signals that nobody did.

### 5. Scale credibility

Drop one concrete scale number early and let the fact do the work.

- "We run outbound for 40 or so B2B companies in this exact space."
- Weak: "We are a leading provider of sales automation." Adjective-driven credibility never lands.
- Why: leading is an opinion and 40 companies is a fact. Only use a number you can defend if asked.

### 6. Gap-hinting CTA

The ask should imply they might be missing something, without condescending.

- Weak: "Worth a look?"
- Strong: "Would you be open to seeing where the gaps are in your current setup?"
- Also strong: "Want me to run a quick comparison against what [peer] is doing?"
- Why: gap and comparison framing implies something unaddressed, and curiosity pulls harder than interest.

### 7. Risk reduction, not discounts

Frame the offer as removing risk rather than giving something away.

- Weak: "We will give you a free trial."
- Strong: "If we ran a teardown of your current sequences, would you want to see the results? No pitch attached."
- Also strong: "30 qualified meetings in 90 days or you do not pay. That is the actual offer."
- Why: a free trial reads as desperate, and a conditional offer reads as confident. Only use the guarantee version if the business will honour it.

## The calibrated-question layer

Each of these has one place in the sequence. Using them everywhere flattens their effect.

### Calibrated questions

What and how questions force a considered answer instead of a reflexive no.

- In practice: "What is the biggest bottleneck when you ramp a new rep?"
- Where: the step 1 CTA or the step 2 opener.
- Common mistake: asking why. Why makes people defensive, what and how make them think.

### Accusation audit

Name the objection before they do, which defuses it.

- In practice: "You probably get a dozen of these a week, so I will keep this to three sentences."
- In practice: "I know the last thing you need is another vendor promising the world."
- Where: the step 1 opener, especially for over-prospected personas.
- Common mistake: over-apologising. One sentence is enough, and two starts to sound insecure.

### Loss aversion

People protect what they have more strongly than they pursue what they do not.

- In practice: "Most teams at your stage lose two or three qualified deals a month to slow follow-up, and it never shows up in the CRM as a loss."
- Where: the agitate section.
- Common mistake: manufacturing urgency. Real loss aversion runs on real numbers, and "act now" runs on nothing.

### That's-right moments

Describe their situation accurately enough that they agree before noticing they have agreed.

- In practice: "You have a strong product and customers love it, but growth has come from word of mouth, and that stops scaling at a certain point."
- Where: the personalized opening or the agitate section.
- Common mistake: being too generic. "Your industry is competitive" produces "so what" rather than "that's right".

### No-oriented questions

People feel safer saying no than yes, so frame the ask so a no still moves things forward.

- Subject: `have you given up on outbound?`
- CTA: "Would it be a terrible idea to send the teardown over?"
- CTA: "Is this a bad time to bring this up?"
- Where: subject lines, and the step 3 or step 4 CTA.
- Common mistake: negative framing that reads as passive aggression. "You probably do not care about pipeline" is over the line.
